Shipping #1: it takes a clear position on a phrase everyone uses, and the second line names the target.

```
If one person approves 400 AI recommendations a day, that isn't oversight. They're a rubber stamp with a login.

I think "human in the loop" has become the phrase we use to stop asking harder questions.

Here's how it usually goes. The model makes a recommendation. A person is told to review it. The queue grows. By week 3 they're clicking approve. The system is right most of the time, and there are 60 more cases waiting.

Researchers have studied this for 20+ years. It's called automation bias, and it means the loop is still there on the diagram, but nobody is in it.

Australia's AI Ethics Principles include contestability and accountability. Neither one is met by putting a person in front of a button.

What I'd design for instead:

Give the reviewer the reasons, not just the answer. If they can't see why the model said yes, they can't disagree with it.

Size the queue so review is possible. If one person can't look properly at every case, sample, tier by risk, or slow the automation down.

Measure how often reviewers overturn the model. If that number is zero for months, you don't have oversight. You have a delay.

Give the affected person a way to challenge the outcome, and make sure it reaches someone who can change it.

Human oversight is a design decision. If it isn't in the architecture, it's only in the slide deck.

If you looked at your AI review queue today, how often does a reviewer actually say no?

#ResponsibleAI #AIEthics #AIGovernance
```

Shipping #21: a reusable template drives saves, and it ties the TOGAF study to AI accountability.

```
Most architecture principles change nothing. Here's the 4 part template from TOGAF that fixes that. Steal it.

I'm studying for TOGAF 10 at the moment, and this is the part I'd take into any team tomorrow, certified or not.

A principle like "we value security" or "data is an asset" sounds fine and decides nothing. Nobody loses an argument to it. Ever.

TOGAF writes each principle in 4 parts:

1. Name. Short and easy to remember.

2. Statement. One sentence, clear enough that two people would apply it the same way.

3. Rationale. Why it matters to the business, in plain words.

4. Implications. What has to change, what it will cost, and what you can no longer do.

Number 4 is where it gets real. If the implications don't make anyone uncomfortable, the principle isn't doing any work.

Here's one I'd write for AI:

Name: Every AI decision has an owner.
Statement: Any AI-assisted decision that affects a person has a named role accountable for it.
Rationale: When a decision is wrong, someone has to explain it and fix it.
Implications: No AI use case goes live without that role signed off. Some pilots will wait. Ownerless "innovation" projects stop.

Write 1 principle this way this week and test it on your next design review. Which principle in your current set would fail the implications test?

#TOGAF #EnterpriseArchitecture #ArchitectureGovernance
```
