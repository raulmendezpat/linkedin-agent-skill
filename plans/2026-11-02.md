Shipping #1: it takes a clear position and the second line owns the risk.

```
An AI review board that meets once a month is not governance. It's a queue.

I'll probably annoy a few people with this one.

Most AI governance I've seen is set up the way we did architecture governance 15 years ago. A committee, a 12-page template, a monthly meeting. Teams fill in the form, wait 3 weeks, and get a decision on a design that has already moved on.

Then everyone's surprised when people route around it.

AI systems change faster than that. A prompt gets edited on a Tuesday. The model version changes on a Friday. A board that meets on the first Monday of the month sees none of it.

What I think works better: put the rules where the work happens. Risk tiering at intake, so low risk use cases go straight through. Guardrails and logging in the CI/CD pipeline. Human review kept for decisions that affect people.

NIST's AI RMF and ISO/IEC 42001 both treat governance as something you run continuously, not something you attend.

The board still matters. Its job is to set the rules and handle the few cases that need judgement, not read every form.

Governance that slows every team down doesn't make AI safer. It just makes it invisible.

Where does your AI governance actually sit: in a meeting, or in the pipeline?

Shipping #21: the reader can copy it today, which drives saves.

```
Before I let an AI agent touch a real workflow, I write 1 page. 5 questions. Here they are.

I build small agents in my own time, on Claude and Python, and this page has saved me from my own bad ideas more than once.

1. What decision does the agent make? Not "help with" or "support". The actual decision, in 1 sentence.

2. Who owns that decision when it's wrong? A named role, not "the system".

3. What can it call? List every tool, API and data source. If it isn't on the list, it can't touch it.

4. What happens when it fails? Timeouts, bad JSON, a model that refuses. Write the fallback down, including the point where a person takes over.

5. How will you know what it did? Log the inputs, the tool calls and the output, so you can explain any single run to someone who wasn't there.

If I can't answer question 2, I stop. That one tells me whether I've got an architecture problem or an accountability problem.

None of this needs a new framework. It's the same discipline as any integration design on AWS or Azure, applied to something that makes choices.

Copy the 5 questions into your next design doc. Which one would your current AI project struggle with?
