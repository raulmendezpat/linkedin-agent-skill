Hooks

Shipping #1: the belief is common right now, and the second half of the line gives a concrete alternative instead of just disagreeing.

```
Everyone wants an AI agent. Most of the use cases I see need a 5 step workflow with 1 model call in the middle.

I know. That's not what the conference slides say.

An agent decides its own next step. It picks a tool, reads the result, then picks again, as many times as it thinks it needs to. That's powerful when the path really can't be known in advance.

Most business processes aren't like that. Triage a request, pull a record, draft a reply, hand it to a person to check, close it. 5 steps, all known. Only step 3 needs judgement from a model.

Build that as an agent and you pay for it. 10 model calls where 1 would do, so more cost and more latency. Dozens of paths to test instead of 1. And a much harder answer when someone asks why it did what it did.

Build it as a workflow instead. The model does the 1 step it's good at, and the other 4 are ordinary integration (queues, APIs, retries, logs) of the kind we've been running on AWS and Azure for years without anyone calling it innovation. Boring works.

The guide Anthropic published in December 2024 on building effective agents makes the same point. Start with the simplest pattern that works. Add autonomy only when it earns its place.

Here's my rule. If I can draw the steps on a whiteboard in 2 minutes, it's a workflow, and the agent has to justify itself.

What's 1 "agent" in your organisation that's really a workflow in disguise?

```

Shipping #21: a template the reader can use today drives saves, and it turns transparency into something concrete.

```
Here's the 6 line note I'd want for any AI system that makes decisions about people. Steal it.

Transparency gets talked about a lot. It rarely gets written down in a form a normal person can read.

So write it down. One page, plain English, 6 lines:

1. What it does. One sentence. "It recommends which applications to check first", not "it uses machine learning to optimise outcomes".

2. What it doesn't do. The decisions it never makes on its own.

3. What it uses. The data it looks at, and anything it isn't allowed to use.

4. Who is accountable. A role, not a vendor and not "the system".

5. How a person can challenge it. Where to go, and what happens next.

6. When it was last checked. The date, and what was tested.

Line 2 is the one teams skip. I think it's the one people care about most, because knowing a person makes the final call changes how the whole thing feels.

Australia's AI Ethics Principles include transparency, contestability and accountability. This page is what those words look like on an ordinary Tuesday.

Model cards do a similar job for engineers. This is the version for the person on the other end.

Write it for one AI system this week. Which line was hardest to fill in?
```

```
Good AI governance writes the rules. Great AI governance gets them into the architecture.

That gap is the problem I like working on most.

The frameworks aren't the hard part anymore. NIST's AI RMF, ISO/IEC 42001 and Australia's AI Ethics Principles are all public. Most organisations can write a decent AI policy in a month.

The hard part comes next. Turning "AI decisions must be explainable" into a logging design. Turning "high risk uses need human review" into a queue someone can actually work. Turning "accountability" into a named role on a design document.

That's architecture work. Here's where I help:

Taking an AI principle and writing it as something a design review can test.

Designing AI and data solutions on AWS and Azure where the evidence exists before anyone asks for it.

Getting risk, security, data and delivery teams to agree on one decision, and writing it down so it sticks.

The background behind it: 25+ years in ICT across banking, credit data, consulting and Queensland government. A Master of Data Science from UQ, UNSW study in AI strategy and leadership, and TOGAF 10 in progress. I work in English and Spanish.
```
